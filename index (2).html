What carries across the work

I am most useful where the product problem is still partly hidden inside an existing workflow, archive, or expert habit.

Read the evidence before asking for the ideal process

Both projects began with archives rather than polished requirements: years of timestamps, remarks, recipes, and version histories. Those traces showed the cadence and exceptions of the real work before the interface was designed.

Make expertise reachable without removing judgement

The goal is not to automate away the expert. It is to move tacit knowledge into a system a less-experienced colleague can inspect and use, while keeping the final judgement with the person responsible for the outcome.

Calm by default, explicit when action is needed

Operational software should not demand attention continuously. I reserve visual emphasis for exceptions, thresholds, uncertainty, or decisions that change what happens next.

Design the contract, not only the screen

When research produces field names, ranges, validation rules, and state logic, I carry those decisions into the frontend and API contract. That shortens the distance between what was learned and what actually ships.

AI is part of how I design, not only what I design

I use Claude Code daily for prototyping and frontend work, moving between Figma and production components as ideas mature rather than treating handoff as a one-way step.

Behaviour first, then interface

My doctoral research at Radboud University was in behavioural science, focused on a question that still shapes how I design: what people perceive can matter more to their behaviour than what objectively exists around them.

I studied how people's perception of their environment influenced what they did in it, using survey research, structural equation modelling, and mediation analysis. The work taught me to look beyond what a system contains and ask what a person actually notices, understands, and believes they can act on.

That question now sits at the centre of my product work. Before designing an interface, I look for the behaviour underneath it: the shortcuts people already take, the information they trust, the decisions they hesitate over, and the signals they may miss.

Then I decide what the interface should make obvious, quiet, editable, or impossible to ignore.
